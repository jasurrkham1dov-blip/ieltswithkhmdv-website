Part 4 – History of Paint

Part 4. First, you have some time to look at questions 31 to 40. Now listen carefully and answer questions 31 to 40.

Lecturer: Good morning, everyone. Today we’re looking at the history of paint. As you’re about to see, there’s a lot to be said about it, so today I’m just going to focus on ancient techniques for producing paint. We’ll deal with prehistoric times to about the end of the Roman Empire.

So let’s start with the prehistoric era. As I’m sure you’re all aware, it’s impossible to determine exactly when colours were first used by humans in art, but what we do know is what those first colours were made of and how they were applied. Researchers have determined that the first colours came from clay, which was most likely used to create marks on the 31face. Of course, the art from this paint, as we’ll call it, wasn’t permanent, so it’s difficult for us to know much more about what it was used for or who used it.

However, some evidence of ancient paint has been found in Australia. There, scientists have discovered early forms of paint in drawings inside caves. Of course, as you’d expect, the colours used in these drawings are simple, earthy colours, and people would have had to make them from naturally occurring materials around them, such as charcoal from their fires.

One of the problems with the ancient paint was that it didn’t last long. It was only temporary, because it faded easily or got blown away by the wind or washed off in rain. It wasn’t until people introduced other elements, such as animal fats, to the paint that this problem was solved. In studying this ancient paint, scientists have determined that another popular additive, something that you wouldn’t have expected at all, was 32honey. Surprising, perhaps, but there you go. It worked well enough for us to be able to enjoy their artwork today.

With the advent of more permanent colours, paints could be transported long distances. This is important because there is evidence to suggest that colours were valuable and may have been used at one time or another as a type of 33money in some of these early societies.

The next important stage in paint’s history was in the time of the ancient Egyptian Empire. With wealth and power, monuments were constructed and decorated, and also a large number of 34temples. This led to the development of a wider range of paint. Interestingly, we now know that one of the problems with much of the paint used at that time, and actually for centuries after, was that it was 35toxic, though it is unlikely that the artists of the time were aware of that.

The Egyptian era saw the introduction of many new colours. Egyptian blue is one of the best known colours of that time. It is thought to be the first paint colour made by man. It is not a colour that exists in 36nature. Craftsmen of the time had perfected the art of making this unusual dark blue colour, and it’s a colour that historians strongly identify with Egyptian culture at this time. The colour Egyptian blue was produced by crushing up blue 37glass very finely. This colour was very popular in Egypt, and it was common for the wealthy to use it as decoration on their tombs.

Red earth, another paint colour that emerged at that time, was also created by a crushing process. This colour was produced when minerals that were red in colour were ground into a very fine 38powder.

The Roman Empire also contributed a lot to the expanding range of paint colours available to artists. They extracted all sorts of colours from a wide variety of plants, but perhaps the most interesting colour of Roman times was a paint pigment called dragon’s blood. As its name suggests, it was a red-coloured pigment. It was incredibly popular at the time, most likely due to the belief that the paint was a mixture of the blood from fights between dragons, which people really believed in then, and 39elephants.

At the time, the idea of dragons was very exotic and exciting, and the colour was much sought after. However, of course, the paint colour was not actually from any animal at all. It came from the tree trunks of a species of tree from Southeast Asia, or more specifically, the 40gum from the trunks of those trees. Not nearly as exciting as dragons, sadly.

That is the end of Part 4. You now have one minute to check your answers to Part 4. That is the end of the listening test.
