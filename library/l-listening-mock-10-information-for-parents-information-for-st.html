Part 4 – Urban biodiversity

Green marks show where each answer is heard.

Today, I want to talk about urban biodiversity, that is, the amazing variety of plant and animal life that we find in cities throughout the world.

Cities are providing new opportunities for wildlife through habitats provided in spaces such as parks and empty undeveloped lots. With some help from humans, species can be preserved despite living close to people. Sometimes, species survive more successfully than in rural areas, where they may be threatened by 31hunting, though this is increasingly regulated by governments.

Another threat they face in rural areas is the ever-encroaching spread of farms and suburban housing, which reduces natural habitats significantly. Once you start to look, you will be amazed by the variety of life which exists side by side with people in cities. Look at the wildlife which share our buildings.

There are moss and lichens on older buildings, and particularly numerous are the 32insects which find breeding places and shelter there. And urban environmentalists have for a long time been aware of the particular attraction of garden 33walls made of wood, stone, brick, and even concrete, which provide ideal spots for smaller plant and animal species to thrive. Over time, many species that were initially crowded out from their habitats return to the sites of their original inhabitation, now in the heart of cities.

This is particularly true in the case of birds. London is today home to the peregrine falcon, a bird once thought close to extinction there, but now doing very well. This magnificent falcon has actually learned how to search for food by 34night when its urban prey is more likely to be active, instead of its traditional forays for food by day.

And the seagull, now living in cities, provides safety for its offspring from urban predators by building its 35nest using trash from the streets below.

This adaptation of urban animals can be compared with the adaptation of humans themselves about 10,000 years ago, when they developed as a society from being hunter-gatherers to a much more geographically stable system dependent upon 36agriculture. This process led initially to lower life expectancies, in part because the diet of these early humans became less varied and thus, ironically, less healthy.

In addition, as humans began to live in ever more dense population centres, 37disease increased significantly. But over several thousand years, new human gene variants slowly allowed them to cope with these threats to their very existence.

Special urban biodiversity projects are being set up in a number of big cities across the world, including Toronto and Singapore, where inhabitants are being encouraged to create a record of the biodiversity in their environment.

Glasgow has recently launched a plan for volunteers from interested community groups to record information using 38surveys to determine residents’ ideas about how to increase local biodiversity. Gathering such data has led to a variety of schemes promoting wildlife. In the UK, a group called Biodiversity Strategy is conducting a special program to encourage the introduction of thousands more 39trees in urban areas because of their particular suitability as shelter for large numbers of wildlife species.

Certain animals, including, most famously, rats and pigeons, have adapted incredibly successfully to cities and, in fact, have done so well in some instances that they have become nuisances and a hazard to 40health. Likewise, among plants, some weeds often adjust well to urban life, much to the annoyance of landscape specialists and gardeners. Many urban dwellers will tolerate nature only if they can control it and if it is relatively clean, not messy or random.

In other words, if wildlife is not truly wild.
