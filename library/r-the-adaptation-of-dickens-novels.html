The adaptation of Dickens' novels for film

When cinema emerged in the mid-1890s, it inherited the visual appeal of many of its forerunners, such as the photograph, the circus, the waxwork exhibition and the theatre. But it was the written word in fiction that had the most significant effect on film, with the mass audiences, its plots, and its techniques of narration. And from no other author did film inherit so much as from Charles Dickens (1812–1870).

Since 1897, when the American Mutoscope Company showed Dickens’ Death of Nancy Sykes on screen, more films have been made of works by Dickens than of any other author. There are over 130 Dickens films on record, and only two novels (Dracula, by Bram Stoker and Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde, by Robert Louis Stevenson) beat Dickens’ Oliver Twist and A Christmas Carol for the status of most-filmed piece of fiction in history. There are numerous reasons for this popularity. Part of the appeal of Dickens is the writer’s attraction for all ages, from young learners exploring books for the first time, to serious, literary-minded adults. Another reason is the author’s contemporary status as a celebrity, and the sheer familiarity of his stories. Also, many of Dickens’ works had been adapted for the theatre long before moving pictures became common. A further reason for filming Dickens’ stories is purely economic: because of the time limits imposed by copyright law, after 1920 none of Dickens’ works were protected by copyright and they could thus be reproduced as films free of charge. Finally, though Dickens’ characters and plots are very British, they seem to ring true universally. Films of his work have been produced in cultures as diverse as Scandinavia (an adaptation of his Little Dorrit in 1914) and Portugal (an updated version of Hard Times in 1988).

In exploring any aspect of the history of films, one must bear two things in mind. First, that we cannot today know the whole picture: 80 percent of all feature films of the silent era (1895–1928), and a number of later films, have been deliberately or accidentally destroyed, so the historical evidence is highly fragmentary. Secondly, all adaptation is in some way a reflection of the period in which it is carried out.

The very first cinema programmes of the 1890s consisted largely of silent versions of variety show acts like dancers and magicians, but such scenes of one minute or less were hardly the stuff of literary adaptation. Within a decade, the new medium began to aspire to ‘art’ status and looked to literature for material, techniques and respectability. In America, for example, the Vitagraph Company laid claim to Dickens’ character Nancy in an early long film of 1909. In 1922, 23 percent of film output in Britain was based on novels. In 1935 it was 26 percent, and by 1939 it was 54 percent. Although the British film industry never achieved the gigantic success of the American industry based in Hollywood, it has also produced great cinematic works and inspired world-famous directors.

From the early days of British film, there was a great urge within the British cinema industry to adapt Dickens’ work. This was despite the challenges that his language and broad scope presented to adaptations that lacked the power of the spoken word and ran for only one hour or so. The first feature film made in Britain was of Shakespeare’s Henry VIII in 1911 and the second was the Hepworth Company’s 1912 58-minute version of Dickens’ novel Oliver Twist. The film was no less violent than Victorian stage versions of the novel, which were frequently banned. It was the start of a successful collaboration between Cecil Hepworth and his director, Thomas Bentley. Their film adaptations illustrate Dickens’ appeal and conceptual importance for film-makers.

It is true, however, that the Hepworth Company’s film of David Copperfield (1913) had significant shortcomings. The written dialogue projected onto the screen in the absence of sound was so long that it distracted from the visual story, and some of the technical camera work was decidedly awkward. Nevertheless, the use of man-made sets was innovative and had a far-reaching influence on subsequent set design in films.

Full-page newspaper advertisements for the film claimed that it had been shot in the locations made famous by the author in his novel. This was, of course, not strictly true, but it was a claim only a British company could make. The company was exploiting perhaps Britain’s most valuable advantage with regard to filming Dickens’ novels: access to the historical locations where his stories were based. Hepworth and Bentley followed David Copperfield with three more Dickens adaptations: The Old Curiosity Shop (1914), The Chimes (1914), and Barnaby Rudge (1915), for which 1780s London was recreated in a giant set constructed in a rural field 40km outside the city. Barnaby Rudge attracted the interest of Hollywood, and thus Bentley became the first British talent to be employed by the film studios there.
