The history of lighting

Candlelight

We forget how painfully dark the world once was. A candle – a good candle – provides barely a hundredth of the illumination of a single 100 watt light bulb, and yet candles provided most of the world's artificial illumination before the arrival of electricity. Candles were of two types – tallow and wax. Tallow, made from animal fat, had the great advantage that, as it could be made at home as a by-product of any animal which also supplied food to eat, it was plentiful and cheap. But tallow candles melted extremely quickly and had to be frequently replaced, they burned with an uneven light, and also they smelled very unpleasant. Far superior in all these respects were candles made of beeswax. These gave a steadier light, but they cost about four times as much, and tended to be used only on special occasions.

Oil lamps

Before the advent of electricity, for those who could afford it, oil lamps were the most efficient option, but the cost was prohibitive for most people, and oil lamps were dirty and needed cleaning daily. Even over the course of an evening, a lamp might lose 40% of its illuminating power as its glass chimney accumulated an increasingly thick layer of black soot.

Until the late 18th century, the quality of oil lighting had remained unchanged for some 3,000 years. But in 1783 a Swiss physicist named Ami Argand invented an oil lamp that increased lighting levels dramatically by ensuring more oxygen reached the flame. The Argand lamp also came with a knob, a novelty that allowed the user to adjust the flame's level of brightness. The best oil light was produced by whale oil, and the best type of whale oil was spermaceti from the head of the sperm whale. However, getting such oil was a difficult and dangerous business and by the 1850s, a gallon (4.55 litres) of whale oil sold for $2.50 – half the average weekly wage.

Gas lighting

For the well-to-do in many large cities, gas was an additional option from about 1820. Mostly, however, it was used in businesses and for street lighting, and didn't become common in homes until closer to the middle of the century. Gas had many drawbacks. Those who worked in gas-supplied offices or visited gas-lit theatres, for example, often complained of headaches and nausea. To minimise that problem, gas lights were sometimes erected outside windows to light the interior. If installed indoors, they tended to blacken ceilings, discolour fabrics, corrode metal and leave greasy marks on every horizontal surface. Gas also needed some care in use. Most gas-supply companies reduced gas flow through their pipes during the day when demand was low. So anyone lighting a gas jet during the day had to open the tap wide to get a decent light. But later in the day as the companies increased the gas flow, the light could flare dangerously, scorching ceilings or even starting fires, wherever someone had forgotten to turn down the tap.

Gas had one irresistible advantage, however. It was bright – at least compared with anything else the pre-electric world knew. The average room with gas was 20 times brighter than without it. It provided wonderful overall illumination. At home in the evening, people read more and stayed up later. It is no coincidence that the mid-19th century saw a sudden and lasting boom in newspapers, magazines, books and sheet music.

Electricity

One natural phenomenon known to science by the early 19th century had the promise to eliminate most of the dangers and shortcomings of candles and gas: electricity. Electricity had great potential, but it was hard to devise practical applications for it. Then, in 1846, rather unexpectedly, a man named Frederick Hale Holmes, patented an electric arc light. Holmes' light was made by generating an electric current and forcing it to jump between two carbon rods, the result being a blindingly bright light. Arc lighting, however, was never a huge success because it was expensive and needed constant attention to run smoothly. In the early 1870s, Hermann Sprengel, a German chemist working in London, came up with a device that proved crucial to making electrical household illumination possible. Sprengel found that he could decrease the air in a glass chamber to one-millionth its normal volume. This meant that light inside the chamber could shine for hundreds of hours. All that was needed was to find a suitable material for the 'filament' which would glow inside it.

The most determined search was undertaken in 1877 by Thomas Edison, already America's most renowned inventor. After trying out a large number of possibilities from the far corners of the world, Edison finally hit upon a piece of thread which had been carbonised.

This treatment seemed to work successfully and the light would burn for many hours. When Edison's first practical installation came it was lastingly significant. He wired a whole district of New York City and, on the afternoon of 4 September 1882, Edison operated a switch that illuminated 800 electric bulbs in 85 businesses that had signed up to his scheme. By modern standards those first lights were pretty feeble, but to people of the time the electric light appeared miraculous.

The Black-Footed Ferret

Evolution and Classification

Ferrets evolved in Europe about 3 to 4 million years ago from small, weasel-like ancestors. Today, only three ferret species remain on Earth: the European polecat, the Siberian polecat, and the black-footed ferret, the only one native to North America. Scientists believe these ferrets migrated from Siberia into North America across the Bering Land Bridge during the Ice Age. Over many generations, they adapted to a life intertwined with prairie dogs, gradually developing specialized traits suited to the open grasslands. Fossil evidence and genetic data indicate that the black-footed ferret diverged from its Siberian relative between 0.5 and 2 million years ago. Within the Mustelidae family – which also includes minks, badgers, martens, wolverines, and domestic ferrets – it represents one of the most specialized members, perfectly evolved for hunting in the prairie ecosystem.

Physical Features and Behavior

The black-footed ferret is a slender, agile animal, typically measuring 45–60 centimeters in length, including a 13–15 cm tail, and weighing around 0.7–1.1 kilograms. Males are slightly larger than females. Their fur is yellowish-buff with black markings on the feet, face mask, and tail tip, giving them a bandit-like appearance. Short legs, large rounded ears, and long claws are physical adaptations for digging and moving through underground tunnels.

Ferrets are nocturnal and solitary creatures, spending most of their time below ground in prairie dog burrows. They communicate using a variety of sounds – chatters, barks, and hisses – especially during the breeding season. Young ferrets, known as “kits,” are playful and often engage in a curious hopping movement called the “ferret dance,” which serves both as a form of play and a way to practice hunting behavior.

Reproduction and Life Cycle

Breeding occurs in early spring, usually from March to April. After a gestation period of about 42 days, females give birth to an average of 3–4 kits, though litter sizes can range from one to ten. The young are born blind and hairless and depend entirely on their mother. Females raise their offspring alone in abandoned prairie dog burrows, providing warmth and protection from predators.

Kits develop fur and markings after three weeks, open their eyes at five weeks, and begin exploring above ground at around ten weeks of age. By late summer, they start to hunt independently, although they may remain near the mother until autumn. In the wild, black-footed ferrets live for one to three years, while individuals in captivity can survive up to nine years due to stable food sources and protection from predators.

Habitat and Diet

Black-footed ferrets are native to the vast shortgrass and mixed-grass prairies of North America, once ranging from southern Canada through the central United States to northern Mexico. These grasslands were historically dominated by prairie dog colonies, which provided both food and shelter for the ferrets.

However, large-scale agricultural expansion and prairie dog eradication in the 19th and 20th centuries drastically reduced available habitat. Less than two percent of the ferret's original range remains today, mostly in isolated reintroduction sites.

Prairie dogs make up more than 90 percent of the black-footed ferret's diet. Ferrets hunt primarily at night, entering burrows to ambush their prey with a quick throat bite. An adult ferret consumes roughly one prairie dog every three days and stores excess food underground, a behavior known as caching. This minimizes the need to travel above ground, where they risk being attacked by owls, coyotes, or badgers.

Decline and Rediscovery

Native American tribes were the first humans to encounter black-footed ferrets, often using their skins, bones, and furs in rituals or for medicinal purposes. Many tribes regarded the animal as a sacred symbol of agility and survival. Despite their significance in Indigenous culture, black-footed ferrets remained elusive to Western scientists. The species was first formally described in 1851, yet few people had ever seen one in the wild. By the mid-20th century, habitat loss and the widespread extermination of prairie dogs caused a dramatic population collapse. By the 1950s, the species was presumed extinct. A small population was unexpectedly discovered in 1964 in South Dakota, prompting conservationists to start a captive breeding program. Unfortunately, disease and a lack of breeding success led to the death of all individuals by 1979. The black-footed ferret was declared extinct once again.

Then, in 1981, a ranch dog named Shep in Meeteetse, Wyoming, brought home a dead ferret, sparking renewed interest. Biologists soon found a surviving wild colony, leading to the establishment of the Black-Footed Ferret Recovery Program. This rediscovery provided crucial insights into the species’ ecology and behavior.

Conservation and Recovery Efforts

From the Meeteetse population, 18 surviving ferrets formed the basis of a new captive breeding program managed by the Wyoming Game and Fish Department and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. Since 1991, ferrets bred in captivity have been successfully reintroduced into the wild across several U.S. states and parts of Canada and Mexico. Reintroduction programs have involved collaboration among federal and state agencies, Native American tribes, conservation organizations, and private landowners. Scientists continuously monitor released ferrets to ensure genetic diversity and resistance to diseases such as canine distemper and sylvatic plague, both of which previously decimated populations.

While challenges remain, particularly habitat fragmentation and disease, the black-footed ferret is considered one of the most remarkable comeback stories in North American wildlife conservation. With continued protection, vaccination programs, and habitat restoration, experts believe that full recovery of the species is possible within the coming decades.

The geography of taste buds

Scientific researchers mapping the geography of the human tongue have finally succeeded in locating and identifying the sensors for sour tastes

Scientists have long known that the surface of the tongue is covered with taste buds: onion-shaped bundles of cells that stand guard for the digestive system. Long before our modern world of supermarkets and restaurants, taste buds served as a crucial tool to maintain the daily existence of our ancestors. The bitter flavour of a wild almond told a prehistoric human foraging in African forests that the nut was poisonous, and they should spit it out. A sweet fruit, though, was safe to consume, and full of energy from carbohydrates. The presence of lions and other man-eating predators also added urgency to meal choices. 'If the thing didn't taste good, you might decide it's not worth it to be out in the open and at risk,' says Steve Miller, a taste researcher at the University of Maryland School of Medicine.

Much of what we 'taste' in everyday life is actually the work of our nose. The tongue can distinguish only five basic flavour sensations – sweet, sour, bitter, salt and umami, the 'savoury' taste of meat and cheese which indicates protein. Sour taste comes from acidic compounds, and, like bitter taste, it can be a sign to leave decomposing meat or vegetables. Unpleasant tartness in unripe fruits comes from too much citric acid. This is how a tree or shrub keeps hungry diners at bay until its seeds are mature enough to travel – via the diner's digestive system – to a new home.

Unlike animals, humans have acquired a taste for tartness. In the case of the Japanese pickle called ‘umeboshi’, the plums are harvested when still green and sour, before the tree fills them with sugar and turns them into a tantalising snack for birds and animals. Sour cream, yoghurt, lemonade, gorgonzola cheese, the German fermented cabbage ‘sauerkraut’ and other tart treats are eaten every day by millions of people.

Only a decade ago, popular myth held that separate areas of the tongue were responsible for each taste. Referred to as the ‘mouth map’, the concept probably originated with a German text mistranslated into English at the beginning of the 20th century. As recently as 1996, ‘mouth map’ diagrams still appeared in university neuroscience textbooks.

By the late 1990s, however, scientists had learned enough about molecular biology to turn their attention seriously to taste. They had discovered that thousands of taste buds cover the tongue, each containing 50 to 100 taste cells. Each cell has two poles: one end covered with taste receptors projecting from the tongue's surface, and one end inside the tongue that connects to the brain via nerves. When a person bites into a lemon, acidic molecules from the food bump into acid-sensitive taste receptors, activating a taste cell, which sends a ‘sour’ message to the brain.

The details of this process were long a matter of debate. In line with the ‘mouth map’ concept, some scientists believed that the taste buds in different areas of the tongue were filled with cells of only one type: the tip with sweet-sensing cells, the back with bitter-sensing cells, and so on. Others were not convinced and further suggested the location of cells was not an issue because each cell could sense all five tastes. However, no one had identified any of the taste receptors, so which cells responded to which tastes was unknown.

Charles Zanker, professor of biology at the University of California, San Diego, and others set out to solve this problem with a variety of biological techniques, including engineering mutant mice that lacked certain receptors and could not taste certain tastes. They eventually isolated the first taste receptors – those for bitter and sweet. Each taste cell, they found, is covered with receptors for only one taste. A sweet cell, for example, is only turned on by sweet molecules because it only has sweet receptors. This dispelled the notion that all cells could sense all tastes.

The scientists also assembled more evidence against the 'mouth map' myth when they found that each taste bud contains a full complement of taste cells. 'There are areas of emphasis,’ Miller says, 'but there are not these absolutely divided areas.'

Over time, the researchers identified the receptors for sweet, bitter and umami. But until recently, salt and sour receptors remained elusive. After eight years of this new wave of research into taste, a team led by Hideki Matsunami from Duke University in North Carolina, and Zanker's separate group of Californian scientists, simultaneously published that they had finally found the tongue's tart sensors. This breakthrough has answered some long-standing questions about the tongue's geography.

One surprising finding was that the sour-taste receptors on the tongue are also found in the spinal cord, where they probably monitor the acidity of cerebrospinal fluid. Similarly, sweet and bitter taste receptors have been found in the intestinal tract.

Taken as a whole, the identification of various key taste receptors illuminates a link between the mouth and the brain. It also provides new targets for research. In the past, artificial flavourings were often chance discoveries, made when chemists accidentally tasted a substance, but now experts hope that knowing the identities of taste receptors will allow a more methodical approach to be taken towards developing these additives. New molecules might even be developed to cling to sour taste receptors and block the unpalatable taste of medicines. The finding may also explain how tongues, like fingerprints, are unique, making some people sensitive and others numb to certain tastes.
