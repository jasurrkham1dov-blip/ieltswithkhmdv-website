The moa died out much later than dinosaurs, of course. All trace of moa suddenly disappeared from the fossil records in the 14th century.

Lecturer: Yes, nicely put.

Paul: They were odd-looking, weren’t they? Such small heads compared to their bodies, although no smaller than some South American or Australian birds. And like other flightless birds, their tails were also much diminished. What’s fascinating, though, is that 22no trace of a wing bone has ever been found in moa, which makes them unique among birds.

Lecturer: Yes, good point. So what do you know about moa eggs?

Paul: Well, some people suggest that male moa actually sat on the eggs rather than the females.

Lecturer: Yes, an interesting theory and 23you can’t discount it. It’s possible that females didn’t have sufficient energy to produce such large eggs and then incubate them as well. It’ll be fascinating to see if any future evidence is produced to settle the matter for certain.

Paul: Most females only laid one or two eggs each season, didn’t they? And the incubation period was certainly relatively long, more than two months. This meant that the chicks were sufficiently developed at hatching to 24feed themselves immediately and they would go off for short excursions from the nest sites to do so.

Lecturer: And what do you make of the occasional moa sightings that are still claimed today?

Paul: Oh, it’s 25a bit of harmless entertainment. Every few years someone walking in a remote area of bush tells the papers they suddenly came face to face with a moa. It doesn’t worry me. It’s part of the mythology.

Lecturer: So you think these reports have no credibility at all?

Paul: None whatsoever. The moa is extinct, no question. The reason for their extinction is interesting, too, because a lot of species in New Zealand, like the kiwi bird, are killed by introduced animals. But 26moa were a popular source of protein with the Māori people who first settled in New Zealand. Māori were originally used to a warmer climate and ate a good deal of moa meat in New Zealand as a source of energy.

Lecturer: OK, so will you describe the features of some individual species in your presentation?

Paul: I wanted to mention the North Island moa because plenty of remains have been found to show that although the eggs were of a normal size, this species grew to a greater height than any other. And DNA analysis shows that 27the females towered over the males.

Then the crested moa, the only one with feathers above its eyes. However, we don’t know a lot about it because 28its bones aren’t common, and its eggs are rarer still. It probably lived mostly in the high country.

Lecturer: You could also mention the heavy-footed moa, which was very big, though not especially tall. The second largest egg ever found came from this species.

Paul: Isn’t this the one with the very large olfactory lobes?

Lecturer: Yes, which means it had a powerful sense of smell. One possibility is that 29this species was nocturnal and used these lobes to navigate and find food in the dark.

Finally, the Eastern moa. This one had a very long windpipe, and this was probably used to 30make deeper, more resonant calls than other species.

Paul: What was the purpose of that, do you think?

Lecturer: I’ve read that the Eastern moa lived in very thick bush where eyesight was less useful. That’s not to say its eyesight was bad, but it used sound more frequently for interaction. Well, that should work well, Paul. Good luck with your presentation.

Hello. This is the second in our series of lectures on the subject of recycling. Today, we’re looking at scrap metal, that is, pieces of metal that have been thrown away, or, I suppose, metal which is no longer serving any kind of practical function.

Now, you might think that recycling is a modern phenomenon, but throughout history people have always done this kind of thing, in one way or another. Take the ancient Greeks and Romans. With their scrap metal, copper or bronze, that is, they might turn it into a 31statue. So yes, nothing was wasted. In fact, scrap metal was considered valuable, and the Greeks and Romans used it in a variety of similar ways.

Since then, the use of scrap metal has been turned into a whole industry. In some countries, it’s become a major export. In the US, for instance, dealing in scrap metal started back in the 19th century. This was a time of immense urban migration, and metal dealers knew they could make money out of the metal machinery and tools that had been left behind on a 32farm when whole families had moved away to the city. And some of those dealers are now huge corporations, selling scrap metal to buyers in other countries.

So, why do we need scrap metal today? First of all, there have been positive changes to the 33economy in many countries. And because people have more money, this is driving up demand for goods, and you need metal to make many of those things.

Then there are issues relating to specific types of metal. For example, take copper. The thing is, there are still plenty of sources for copper, but the issue is the 34quality. It’s just not as good as it used to be.

Another reason why we need to locate and reuse old metals is due to an industry that’s really been developing over the last few decades, and of course, it’s 35electronics. Think about all the stuff you’ve bought and then replaced in the last few years. What happens to the things you get rid of? Well, a lot of them just end up being thrown away, and the metal components go to waste.

So, where do people look for scrap metal? Researchers and engineers are now investigating new sources. In the UK, one place they’re looking at is the sides of the roads. That’s because tiny bits of a metal called platinum are emitted by cars, and this can eventually be reused. And they’re also looking at the side of the road because, with some types of 36plants that grow there, it’s been proved that they can absorb metals really effectively.

Sweden is also a country where a lot of work in this area is being done. Sweden used to manufacture a lot of cotton, and so researchers there are looking at old 37factories which used to produce it, but are now closed down. There is so much metal in the walls and in the flooring that could be melted down again. And in the same country, deep below ground, are many, many 38pipes that once provided gas to the surrounding neighbourhood. It’s all there waiting to be excavated, but the trick is how to do this without causing huge disruption to local residents and the traffic flow.
