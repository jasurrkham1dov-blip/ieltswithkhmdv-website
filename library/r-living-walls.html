Living walls – a successful environmental project

Gardens planted on vertical walls are useful as well as decorative

Canadian entrepreneur Alan Darlington, president of Nedlaw Living Walls and holder of a PhD in plant sciences, admits that people may have a point when they say that academics don't necessarily make good businessmen. While good ideas come up in university labs and classrooms all the time, he acknowledges that bringing them to the marketplace requires a different skill set. Yet Dr Darlington's combination of two existing green technologies into a single breakthrough innovation is not only a laboratory success story, but the heart of what is today a multi-million-dollar business. And, he says, 'I've been a part of the whole sequence, from working in the lab to making it work as a business.'

In fact, Dr Darlington's invention was originally designed with space exploration projects in mind. His work at the University of Guelph's Controlled Environments Systems research facility in Ontario, Canada, involved finding methods to recycle the air which astronauts breathe inside a lunar module or space station. He and his team hit upon the idea of integrating two ideas: the use of natural microbes to deal with polluted air, and the use of plants to restore a contaminated environment.

'I guess the big moment was when we saw how we could take those two general ideas and just tweak them slightly, so the system would work indoors,' Dr Darlington explains. 'There were all kinds of mathematics and engineering, but it was really just "If we did this and this — wow".' At the same time, once we started working on the system, we said, 'Well, they're not really making many lunar bases.' So for his idea to work in business terms, he needed to find a better market for it than space was likely to offer. He therefore began to think about using the same technology to remove harmful substances from the air people breathe inside buildings right here on Earth. 'Developing the project required another three years,' says Dr Darlington. 'It took that long to take an idea that was working really well in a laboratory, and translate it into something that would be effective on an industrial scale,' he recalls.

For this phase, the funding he obtained from the Ontario Centres of Excellence (OCE) was key. This organisation provides support to help industries get research done at academic institutions in Ontario. But it may also help recent graduates who have a business idea that originated with the university, by supporting them for up to two years while they get it off the ground.

As a result of this support, Dr Darlington was able to set up a company, Nedlaw Living Walls, in 2001. The first living wall, set up in the University of Guelph's new Humber campus facility in 2004, was followed by another in Toronto the same year. Currently, some 150 living walls are flourishing inside buildings across North America, and they have led to Nedlaw receiving a number of prestigious awards.

The projects advertise themselves better than anything else could, according to Dr Darlington. For example, since they installed their largest-ever living wall at Drexel University's Papadakis Integrated Sciences Building in Philadelphia, the company has been contacted by many local people impressed by what they have seen.

Soaring as high as five storeys in some cases, brilliant with the cascading greenery of foliage, ferns and flowering plants, the living walls provide an environment which can make being indoors seem like a walk through the rainforest and they also save companies a lot of expense. The walls include a system which actively moves air over the plants' leaves, roots and growth medium, eliminating at least 90 per cent of the dust present in the air. So business owners don't have to worry about using ventilation systems to bring in fresh air from outside — they can simply let the living wall clean the air for them. There is some evidence that they may cut down sickness among staff who work in the environment, and businesses which have installed them have also found that they improve productivity by making employees feel more cheerful and motivated.

As well as ten employees based in their Ontario headquarters, Nedlaw employs teams of subcontractors to look after the walls in distant cities. The plants get monthly maintenance visit as part of the package.

'There is a potential for reduced capital and operating expenses,' says Dr Darlington. 'We have the numbers to support what we're doing, so we can actually substantiate our claims about the improvement in air quality and productivity in a building. In fact, as building firms look for new and better ways to construct the spaces where people work and live, a living wall may well be the most obvious sign of such progressive thinking, acting as a marker of dedication to the green movement and green design.'
