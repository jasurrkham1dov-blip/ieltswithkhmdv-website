Part 2 – The story of the Lunette

Green marks show where each answer is heard.

Welcome to this Tourist Information Center podcast on maritime activities in the beautiful city of Victoria. I’m going to talk about boats, past and present. So let’s get started with the Museum of Seafaring Vessels.

While you are there, you’ll be able to see three different historic boats, and you can learn about the journeys their captains and crews took on the high seas. For many visitors, the most memorable boat is not the spectacular yacht or the fishing boat complete with original nets, but a simple wooden canoe called the Lunette.

It was built in the early 1800s from a large red cedar log, and 11in 1901, it was rigged with a 230-square-foot sail and readied for her voyage out of Victoria’s harbor in May of that year. Captained by John Voss.

Voss thought he’d take this wooden boat across the Pacific Ocean over to the Fiji Islands and 12ultimately circle the globe.

The captain asked the journalist Norman Luxton to accompany him, but Luxton abandoned the project after they reached Fiji in October 1901. And after Voss dropped Luxton off in Fiji, he hired an assistant, which unfortunately didn’t work out. 13The assistant somehow lost the only compass, which was necessary to stay on course, and this had serious consequences.

Although he was 1,200 miles from Australia, he made it there safely and, amazingly, on schedule. The Lunette was the smallest deep-water vessel to have ever entered the Sydney harbor.

With a crew of ten men hired in Sydney, Voss kept the journey going for three years and three months and finally arrived in Margate, England. While there, Voss tried to raise funds to have the boat repaired.

After several weeks, the story of the canoe’s long journey spread and, at the time, it became quite famous. Because of this, 14the Lunette was transported from Margate on the coast to London, where it was featured in an exhibition in 1905.

After that, it was sold and passed through a number of London collectors’ hands and then spent some years lying derelict in Brighton, England. But you’ll be pleased to know that didn’t last long.

Through the efforts of concerned residents of Victoria, 15the Lunette was crated and returned by freighter to its original location there, where restoration was carried out by the Maritime Club.

So, that’s an example of a past voyage. Very different from the ferry rides and boat tours you can take from the city of Victoria these days. You have a wide variety to choose from, so every interest and comfort level can be found.

A good starting ferry, for kids as well as adults, are the small and cute tiny tugs that travel at a leisurely rate across the water. 16The captains even allow the passengers to assist navigating at the wheel.

From the Museum of Seafaring Vessels, you only need to take a short five-minute walk to the nearest harbour ferry stop at the Regent Hotel.

Purposely built for whale watching, the Ocean Magic was designed to be very quiet so as 17not to disturb natural activity of whales and other sea mammals, such as seals and sea lions. Also, the vessel can accommodate large numbers of passengers, which means it reduces the negative impact which the boat would have on the environment if it took more trips.

18If you’re looking to see a wide variety of waterfowl, you should take the Searider. You’ll find it a fully covered and heated vessel, very comfortable. On this amazing trip around the protected shores and cliffs, you’ll view hundreds of puffins, terns and cormorants flying and diving for food.

Now, if you want your own private party, you can actually book your own private charter on the Starship and cruise Victoria’s beautiful inner harbour and local waterfront. 19Your guests can enjoy seafood at our delicious onboard restaurant. The lower deck is equipped with a glass viewing area, so you’ll be able to see many types of plants which cover the rocks with your own eyes.

A ferry, the Porter as it’s commonly called, is known for safety and has a proud maritime history. For everyone’s convenience, 20it carries cars, trucks and buses so travellers can get to their destinations around Victoria easily.

Part 4 – Maori Middens

Green marks show where each answer is heard.

Good morning everyone and welcome to the lecture. Today I’m going to be asking the question: What can archaeologists learn from studying middens? Midden is an old English word meaning a domestic rubbish dump.

By way of providing a local New Zealand example, let me tell you about an ancient midden I excavated recently that was made by the Maori people in this country. It consisted primarily of leftovers from meals, particularly a great quantity of shells from seafood. I also came across something very exciting, 31an axe that had been discarded because it was damaged.

Finds of this kind are of great interest because they tell us about the technologies developed by people in the past. For the same reason, I was most pleased to unearth several fish hooks, another item commonly found in Maori middens. Their hooks were manufactured using 32bone, which is a durable material that can last for hundreds of years in the right conditions.

But why is rubbish like this important, you might well ask. Well, the reason archaeologists are fascinated by rubbish is that it provides information about ordinary life, and written history doesn’t always do that.

Now, having whetted your appetite for middens, I should just mention that they are protected by law. If you wish to make any excavation at a midden, you must first obtain a 33permit from the Historic Places Trust. It is an offence to work on a midden without doing this.

So, how can you find a midden? The best place is on the beach or among sand dunes. Look for low mounds of shells. Some sort of erosion is often important because middens were usually buried. For this reason, it’s not uncommon to find a midden where a 34road is being constructed and the contractors are digging into the land.

Now, in the past, we could only make rough estimates regarding the age of a midden. But with modern radiocarbon dating techniques, archaeologists can accurately ascertain the date it was made. It’s even possible to establish what season of the year the midden was created. This may not sound like useful information at first, but it actually gives us an insight into Maori 35migration, when and where they travelled.

What else can we discover from middens? Well, we can learn a great deal about diet. For example, the study of middens has revealed that the Maori people who lived here before Europeans arrived first concentrated their hunting on giant 36birds, which were rich in fat. But later, as this resource became scarce, they had to find an alternative, so mostly turned to fish, which of course has a much lower fat content. … because it allows us to speculate about how healthy people were, and also what life expectancy the population could look forward to at different stages of its history.

Well, I’m now going to talk about different varieties of middens. The most common type in this country are those made up of shells, which of course are found close to beaches. The other thing to remember with shell middens is that New Zealand has several species of fresh water mussels, as well as the much more common saltwater varieties. This means that small middens made up of these shells are occasionally found beside 37lakes, which can be hundreds of kilometres from the sea, in quite mountainous areas.

Middens containing no shell material have occasionally been discovered in various regions of the South Island, particularly central Otago and on the east coast. However, the thing to emphasise here is that they are extremely 38rare. I don’t know of any new discoveries in the last ten years.

Finally, middens dating from the early European period of New Zealand’s history are also fascinating. How can we distinguish European and Maori middens? Well, certain items are only found in European middens, notably things made of 39glass, which was a material unknown to Maori. But in other respects, the two types are often remarkably similar, suggesting that in many ways the first European settlers adapted to the Maori way of life.

One other distinguishing feature I should just mention is that the two types of middens tend to be found in different locations. Historic European middens are less restricted to the coastlines, and particularly large ones have been left behind in places where 40soldiers had their camps.

Well, that’s the end of the formal part of the lecture.
